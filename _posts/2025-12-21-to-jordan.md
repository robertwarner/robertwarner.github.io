**To Jordan,**

We often find ourselves spiraling into the deep end—asking what consciousness is, what God is, and what the point of all this is. You usually ask the questions that leave me stumped, which is exactly why I love it. It forces me to explore the uncharted corners of my mind.

I’m writing this from the couch, iPad in hand, trying to capture a feeling that is notoriously hard to put into words. As Alan Watts used to say, "The menu is not the meal." But here is my attempt to describe the meal.

All nature, matter, and energy are participating in a game. We tend to look at the universe as a collection of dead parts—rocks, gas, empty space—that somehow accidentally produced life. But I view it differently.

In the same way an apple tree "apples," the universe "peoples."

Everything is conscious in its own way because everything is part of the same continuous process. We see water freezing into ice or the sun fusing hydrogen as mechanical laws, but perhaps that is just the universe arranging itself.

Usually, when we say "consciousness," we mean the spotlight in our heads—the "I" that has a name and a social security number. In this sense, consciousness is merely a trait that has won "survival of the fittest."

But this creates a hallucination. It creates the sensation that we are a "skin-encapsulated ego"—a poor little me inside a bag of skin, confronting an alien universe "out there." This ego clouds our awareness. It makes us forget that we aren't strangers here. We didn't come *into* this world; we came *out* of it, like leaves coming out of a tree.

We are the flow. We are the Dao.

Consider entropy: the scientific idea that the universe is slowly expanding, cooling, and falling into chaos. And yet, look around. Right here, in the middle of all that chaos, we see immense towers of order. Single cells, ecosystems, snowflakes, and the neural network of your brain.

How does this order happen? It doesn't happen through rigid control. The universe isn't *forcing* atoms to bond; it is allowing them to.

The "Dao" is the understanding that the universe has a natural current. When things trust that flow—when they stop resisting and allow the connection—complexity arises effortlessly. Life is what happens when matter surrenders to the flow. It is the result of a "cosmic trust" that if you let go, you won't fall apart; you will fall together.

So, where is God in all this?

God is not a man in the sky. God is not an entity you meet after you die. And God is certainly not a concept you can find in the past or the future, because the past is just memory and the future is just imagination.

God is the **Now**.

God is the pure, unfiltered experience of this exact moment. It is the aliveness you feel before you label it. It is the hum of the room, the sensation of your breath, the presence behind your eyes reading these words.

When you are fully in the Now, you are in the presence of God because you are connecting with the only thing that is actually real. The "point" of the universe isn't to get somewhere else; it is to be fully here. To be so present that the boundary between "you" and "the moment" dissolves.

But if you accept that you are this "Now"—this universe experiencing itself—you have to accept the whole package.

There is a poem by Thich Nhat Hanh called *"Call Me by My True Names"* that captures this perfectly. He writes:

> *"I am the frog swimming happily in the clear pond,*
> *and I am also the grass-snake who, approaching in silence,*
> *feeds itself on the frog."*

It is easy to say "I am God" when you are looking at a sunset. It is much harder to admit that if you are the universe, you are also the snake. You are the hunter as much as the hunted.

When you realize that the "bad" things—or the "bad" people—are just you in a different mask, you stop hating them. You realize that you are the cruelty as much as the kindness.

So, what is the point?

The point isn't just to *know* this intellectually. The point is to choose it.

There is an old speech by David Foster Wallace where he talks about two fish swimming along. An older fish swims by and says, "Morning boys, how's the water?" The two young fish swim on for a bit, and eventually, one looks at the other and goes, "What the hell is water?"

The point is that the most obvious realities are the hardest to see. Our "default setting" is to think that we are the center of the universe. In that mode, we are unconscious. We are asleep to the reality of the oneness we talked about.

True freedom—and true consciousness—is the ability to **control what you think**. To look at the person standing in your way and realize that they, too, are a part of this same "God" or "Dao" or "Now" that you are.

This is the hardest thing in the world to do. It takes effort. But if you can do it—if you can choose love and compassion over the default setting of annoyance and ego—then you are truly awake.
